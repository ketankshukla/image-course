# The right information. The right person.

This is a typography sample, not a website update. Open the neighboring HTML file to judge the intended font sizes and blue palette; Markdown preview styling is controlled by your reader.

A hospital team needs to arrange a patient's journey home. The doctor, the insurer, and the transport company all help with the same task—but they do not need to see the same information.

## Start with a simple question

**What does this person need to do their job?** The transport company needs a pickup location, a destination, and a time. It does not need the patient's diagnosis or complete medical history.

A well-designed system checks permission before sharing anything. It prepares a small, relevant packet instead of sending the entire record and hoping the recipient ignores the private details.

> **The key idea:** permission to receive some information is not permission to receive everything.

## See the journey

Follow the diagram from left to right. Each step answers a different question: who is asking, what may they see, who approved it, and where will it go?

![Four steps in a synthetic hospital discharge: verify, prepare, review, and release.](../case-studies/hospital/assets/01-patient-journey.svg)

**What to notice:** permission is checked before information is prepared and again before it is sent. Approval is not the same as delivery.

### A small, concrete example

The assigned driver receives “Demo Ward A, 3:00 pm, wheelchair assistance.” The diagnosis stays out of the pickup packet. An unrelated transport company receives no patient information.

### Pause and think

Is the pickup location now public? No. It is still private information. One assigned recipient may use it for one approved task; that does not make it available to everyone.

## One thing to remember

**Share what is needed, with the person who needs it, for the task they are allowed to perform.**

## More blues in this family

Keep the main page white and the text dark. Let the blues give different passages a purpose: sapphire for emphasis, powder blue for key ideas, and a quieter ice blue for examples.

| Shade | Color | Reading role |
|---|---|---|
| Midnight navy | #081A33 | Title panels and navigation |
| Sapphire | #245AC4 | Emphasis and selected controls |
| Cornflower | #7094D1 | Decorative accents, not small text on white |
| Powder blue | #EDF4FF | Key-idea backgrounds |
| Ice blue | #F5F8FF | Worked examples and reflection prompts |

The HTML sample includes a reading-time label, 22/24/28px body-size controls, an embedded diagram with an explanatory caption, a quiet worked-example box, an optional reflection answer, and a navy recap panel. Color is reinforced by labels, so meaning does not depend on recognizing a shade.
